# Sports platforms that teach from athlete video — verified deep research (2026-09-19)

Method: 5 search angles → top sources fetched → each claim voted on by 3 adversarial verifiers (2/3 refutes kills it).

## Summary

Four kinds of platform turn an athlete's own footage into instruction: (1) phone-only AI that measures biomechanics, such as OOFSkate for figure skating, HLH Skate Analysis for hockey skating, Sportsbox AI for golf and SwingVision for tennis and pickleball; (2) freemium drill apps with tracking, such as HomeCourt for basketball; (3) side-by-side comparison with elite athletes, such as OOFSkate's U.S. National Team library; and (4) coach video review tools, a segment where Hudl gave up its Technique app in 2021 to focus on team products. The strongest traction evidence is SwingVision's company-reported $4M+ ARR and 20k+ paying subscribers, which is modest for a leading consumer AI sports app. Its recognized revenue for 2024 was only about $2.75M, so the ARR figure is a run-rate. For skating specifically, the national governing body endorsed phone-based AI jump metrics in December 2025 (U.S. Figure Skating's partnership with OOFSkate), which pitches to all levels from Learn to Skate up. In hockey, a youth- and parent-facing AI skating analyzer charges premium prices ($79-99/month). The evidence on learning is positive but weak. Self-video modeling gives a large immediate gain (SMD 0.81, mostly adolescent samples) that is not significant one week later. Video feedback appears better than verbal-only feedback for school-age learners (about ages 9-15), but less so for the youngest beginners. For a kids and teens market, parents are the payers: they spend about $1,016 per child per year on a primary sport, a figure that can absorb a subscription. The costs are COPPA duties for under-13 users (verifiable parental consent, separate consent before sharing data with third parties, and biometric identifiers now counted as personal information) and the need to design for repeated use rather than one-off analysis.

## Findings (ranked by confidence)

### 1. [high] Figure skating: OOFSkate uses AI computer vision on ordinary phone video to measure jump height, rotation speed, airtime and landing quality, with no wearables or multi-camera rigs. On Dec 2, 2025 it formally partnered with U.S. Figure Skating, the national governing body.
- Evidence: The USFS press release says OOFSkate can 'analyze mobile phone video and measure jump height, rotation speed, airtime, and landing quality without requiring wearable sensors.' AP syndication and MIT News (Feb 2026) confirm OOF is an MIT spinout. Both claims passed verification 3-0. The source announces the partnership. It does not show how many people use the app or how accurate it is.
- Vote: 3-0, 3-0 (merged claims 0,1)
- Sources: https://usfigureskating.org/news/2025/12/2/press-releases-us-figure-skating-partners-with-oofskate-to-bring-ai-powered-jump-metrics-to-athletes-nationwide.aspx

### 2. [medium] OOFSkate positions itself for every level, from Learn to Skate USA beginners to world champions, and plans a U.S. National Team library so skaters can compare themselves side by side with elite skaters.
- Evidence: The release says the app serves 'Skaters of every level (from Learn to Skate USA® to world champions)'. It says the library 'will allow' comparisons, in the future tense, so it may not be live yet. The library covers past, current and Olympic-hopeful skaters. Every level is marketing positioning, not proof of use at each level.
- Vote: 3-0
- Sources: https://usfigureskating.org/news/2025/12/2/press-releases-us-figure-skating-partners-with-oofskate-to-bring-ai-powered-jump-metrics-to-athletes-nationwide.aspx

### 3. [high] Hockey: HLH Skate Analysis is an AI-only skating analyzer. It measures 15 biomechanical metrics from uploaded video and gives a 0-100 score in bands from Foundational to Elite, and no human coach reviews the footage. It costs $99/month or $949/year (about $79/month), the first analysis is free with no credit card, there is a 7-day money-back guarantee, and 2- and 3-player bundles cost $1,399 and $1,899 a year.
- Evidence: The live vendor page, checked 2026-09-19, says 'Your video is analyzed entirely by AI, no human ever watches it' and shows the pricing and guarantee terms. Coaches only receive a shareable PDF report card. This is vendor-described, and nothing independent confirms how accurate the metrics are. A separate claim that it targets youth through a parent portal with no mention of COPPA was refuted 0-3.
- Vote: 3-0, 3-0 (merged claims 3,4)
- Sources: https://hlhskateanalysis.com/

### 4. [low] Golf: Sportsbox AI turns phone swing video into 3D biomechanical analysis. It says 500+ coaches and 50,000+ golfers use it.
- Evidence: The homepage quote reads 'Join 500+ coaches and 50,000+ golfers who have upgraded from video to 3D biomechanical analysis.' The figures are self-reported, undated and unaudited, and may include free-tier accounts. The /technology URL returned 404. A claim about its freemium tiers ($0 for 5 swings/month, $9.16/month Premium) was refuted 1-2, although one verifier saw similar tiers on the homepage.
- Vote: 3-0 (figures self-reported)
- Sources: https://www.sportsbox.ai/technology, https://www.sportsbox.ai/

### 5. [medium] Tennis/pickleball: SwingVision, a phone-based AI video app, reported $4M+ ARR, 20k+ paying subscribers and 100+ NCAA D1-D3 college teams during its 2025 Reg CF (Wefunder) raise. This is the strongest traction data point in the category, but its Form C shows 2024 recognized revenue of about $2.75M, up only about 12% from 2023, with a net loss of about $3M.
- Evidence: The Wefunder page and KingsCrowd both give the same figures, though verifiers read both through search snippets because the pages returned 403. The 2024 raise reported 15k+ subscribers and $2.5M+ ARR. The claim that it raised $10M+ from Tennis Australia, Techstars and others was refuted 0-3. The only priced round verified is a $6M Series A, and the company then turned to crowdfunding.
- Vote: 3-0, 3-0 (merged claims 6,21)
- Sources: https://wefunder.com/swingvision-cf-2025, https://kingscrowd.com/swingvision-on-wefunder-2025/

### 6. [high] Basketball: HomeCourt uses a freemium, family-and-team model. Free users get 100+ drills and unlimited shot tracking but only the current and previous month of data. HomeCourt+ costs $69.99/year for up to 4 family profiles or $7.99/month, with a 7-day trial. Team plans cost $1.99 per player per month billed quarterly or $1.50 billed annually, in packs of 10/20/30/50, with larger teams handled by sales.
- Evidence: The live vendor FAQ, checked 2026-09-19, lists the pricing and says 'All users get unlimited shot tracking' and that non-subscribers see only current and previous month data. The FAQ does not say which year the 'Starting in December' data-limit policy began. The freemium claim passed 2-1 and the pricing claims 3-0. Family sharing and per-player team pricing are a model built for youth, where the parent or club pays.
- Vote: 3-0, 3-0, 2-1 (merged claims 7,8,9)
- Sources: https://www.homecourt.ai/faq/subscription

### 7. [high] Failure case: Hudl dropped its self-serve and coach Technique video-analysis app. It sold the app and its user base to Onform in May 2021, discontinued it in summer 2021, and put its investment into team and organization products.
- Evidence: Onform's blog post (May 17, 2021) says 'The Technique app was discontinued in summer 2021' and that Hudl 'chose to focus investment on other products and partner with Onform.' Hudl VP Greg Nelson is quoted in the PR Newswire release. Hudl had acquired the app as Ubersense in 2014. This is a sign that stand-alone individual technique-video apps were less attractive to a team-software company than team and organization products.
- Vote: 3-0, 3-0 (merged claims 19,20)
- Sources: https://onform.com/blog/onform-acquires-hudl-technique/

### 8. [medium] Learning effectiveness: watching video of oneself (self-modeling) gives a large immediate improvement in athletic performance (SMD 0.81, 10 studies, 244 participants, mean age 16.8). The gain was not significant one week after the intervention ended (SMD 0.59, 95% CI -0.09 to 1.27, p=0.09), which suggests sustained, repeated use is needed.
- Evidence: Wang et al. 2026 meta-analysis in Frontiers in Sports and Active Living. Heterogeneity is high (I2=73%), samples are small and mostly adolescent, and only 5 studies had follow-up data. How much of the intervention participants got (the dose) moderated the effect, and age and skill type did not. A separate self-efficacy analysis found SMD 0.62 (mean age 14.5). The finding is a single meta-analysis, which is why confidence is medium.
- Vote: 3-0, 3-0 (merged claims 13,14)
- Sources: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13533993/

### 9. [medium] For child and teen learners (about ages 9-15, school physical education), video feedback combined with verbal feedback, whether from expert modeling or self-modeling, appears more effective than verbal feedback alone. However, the youngest and least experienced learners (for example third graders) did as well or better with verbal feedback.
- Evidence: Mödinger, Woll & Wagner (2021/22) reviewed 11 studies selected from 2,030 screened. Seven found video beat verbal feedback and two found no advantage. Fewer than half ran retention tests, none covered skating, and most used camera setups rather than self-serve phone apps. The authors say 'verbal feedback seems to be the method of choice' for early-stage, very young learners.
- Vote: 3-0, 3-0 (merged claims 15,16)
- Sources: https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12662-021-00782-y

### 10. [medium] Across augmented feedback of all types, most of 24 RCTs (895 participants) reported a learning improvement at retention testing. However, 18 of the 24 were at high risk of bias, none were rated low risk, and the review covered only healthy adults and feedback in general, not video specifically.
- Evidence: The Psychology of Sport and Exercise (2022) review says 'Most studies reported a learning improvement... One study documented a decrease in performance, and two studies reported no learning improvement.' It used RoB2 risk-of-bias ratings and did not pool results in a meta-analysis. The studies disagreed on the best feedback frequency and timing.
- Vote: 3-0, 3-0 (merged claims 17,18)
- Sources: https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1469029222001455

### 11. [high] Youth privacy constraint: the amended FTC COPPA Rule requires separate verifiable parental consent before disclosing children's personal information to third parties such as ad networks. It also adds biometric identifiers to the definition of personal information. Compliance was required by about April 22, 2026. This limits ad and data monetization for apps aimed at children under 13.
- Evidence: The FTC press release (Jan 2025) is a primary source. The rule was published April 22, 2025 and took effect June 23, 2025. It covers only children under 13, not teens aged 13-17. The biometric category covers data used for recognition, such as faceprints and gait patterns, and pose analysis used only for feedback may fall outside it. A child's photo or video, including their image or voice, has been personal information since 2013, so a video app for kids is covered by COPPA either way.
- Vote: 3-0, 3-0 (merged claims 10,11)
- Sources: https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2025/01/ftc-finalizes-changes-childrens-privacy-rule-limiting-companies-ability-monetize-kids-data

### 12. [high] Willingness to pay in youth sports is high and rising. US sports families spent an average of $1,016 per child on the child's primary sport in 2024, up 46% from 2019, which leaves room for a parent-paid subscription.
- Evidence: The Aspen Institute Project Play survey covered 1,848 parents of children aged 6-18 in Nov-Dec 2024. Figures are nominal, and the average may be skewed by high spenders. The average is across all sports; figure skating and hockey are typically well above it, but that was not verified here.
- Vote: 3-0
- Sources: https://projectplay.org/news/2025/2/24/project-play-survey-family-spending-on-youth-sports-rises-46-over-five-years


## caveats
Most traction and product claims come from the vendors themselves: press releases, pricing pages and crowdfunding disclosures. They are unaudited, and SwingVision's ARR is a run-rate that sits well above its recognized 2024 revenue. The research did not verify traction data for the other sports in scope: soccer, baseball, skiing, gymnastics or dance. Several platforms known in these categories, such as CoachNow/Onform's current state and coach-review marketplaces, did not produce claims that survived verification, so coverage of human coach-review marketplaces is thin. The learning-science evidence is small-sample, heterogeneous and at high risk of bias. It shows immediate rather than retained gains and does not come from skating or self-serve phone apps. The youth-specific evidence comes from school physical education and adolescent samples. No verified data covered engagement or retention, or compared youth with adult conversion, for any app. Three claims were refuted and excluded: HLH's youth parent-portal and COPPA positioning, Sportsbox's tier pricing, and SwingVision's $10M+ investor list. COPPA analysis covers the US under-13 threshold only and does not address state laws, GDPR-K or teen-specific rules. Pricing was checked on 2026-09-19 and may change.
